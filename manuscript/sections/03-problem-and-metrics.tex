\section{Problem Setting, Measures, and Analysis}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsection{Feature Selection with Two Objectives}
\label{sec:problem}

A dataset has $d$ features. A candidate subset is a binary vector $x \in \{0,1\}^d$, in which $x_j = 1$ means that feature $j$ is selected, and its size $|x| = \sum_j x_j$ is the number of selected features. We train a classifier on the training part using only the selected features and measure its weighted F1 score on the validation part; we write this score as $s(x)$. The search minimizes $|x|$ and maximizes $s(x)$. A subset dominates another if it is no worse in both objectives and better in at least one. The empty subset cannot be fitted and receives $s = -1$, below every F1 score. Because no other subset is smaller, it is never dominated and can stay in the population, but it is not a usable answer: we define the \emph{returned front} as the non-empty subsets that no other member of the final population dominates. Each of them is fitted once more on the training part, with the same fixed random state and therefore the same model, and scored on validation and test. These final fits are not charged to the search budget.

\subsection{Area Under the Front}
\label{sec:auf}

We summarize a returned front with the area under the front (AUF) introduced with FASTENER~\cite{Koprivec2020}. For a limit of $k$ features, let $q(k)$ be the test F1 of the subset that has the highest validation score among the subsets on the front with at most $k$ features. If the front contains no subset with at most $k$ features, $q(k) = 0$. AUF averages these values over the limits $1, \dots, K$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AUF}_K = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} q(k).
  \label{eq:auf}
\end{equation}
We use $K = 10$ as the primary limit and also report $K = 20$ and $K = d$, the AUF over the whole front. A subset with three features contributes to every limit $k \geq 3$, so a missing subset of exactly four features does not by itself give a zero at $k = 4$. Validation ties are broken deterministically: among subsets of the same size, the first one on the returned front is kept, and across sizes the smaller one. Such ties with different test scores occur at 2.0 to 4.8\% of the reached limits in each group of runs. Because the subset is chosen on validation but scored on test, $q(k)$ need not increase with $k$; it decreases at some limit up to ten in 662 of the 1{,}544 main runs. AUF is therefore the mean of a validation-selected test curve, not the area under a monotone front. The value $q(k) = 0$ is assigned when the front holds no subset within the limit. A returned subset could also have a test F1 of zero, but no reached limit in our runs does, so every zero in our results marks an absent subset. The next subsection separates reach from quality.

\subsection{Reach and Quality at Matched Sizes}
\label{sec:reach}

We say that a run \emph{reaches} the limit $k$ if its returned front contains a subset with at most $k$ features. For one configuration on one dataset, let $r(k)$ be the share of finished seeds whose run reaches $k$, and let $\bar{c}(k)$ be the mean of $q(k)$ over those seeds; $\bar{c}(k)$ is undefined when $r(k) = 0$. We report $R = r(K)$, the share of runs that reach ten features. Averaged over seeds, AUF is then $\overline{\mathrm{AUF}} = K^{-1} \sum_k r(k)\,\bar{c}(k)$: it combines how often a limit is reached with how well the returned subsets predict when it is.

To state how much of the difference $\Delta = \overline{\mathrm{AUF}}_a - \overline{\mathrm{AUF}}_b$ between configurations $a$ and $b$ on one dataset comes from reach, we use the symmetric split of a difference of products:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta = \underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \bigl(r_a(k) - r_b(k)\bigr)\frac{\tilde{c}_a(k) + \tilde{c}_b(k)}{2}}_{\text{reach part } \rho}
  + \underbrace{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{r_a(k) + r_b(k)}{2}\bigl(\tilde{c}_a(k) - \tilde{c}_b(k)\bigr)}_{\text{quality part } \kappa},
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{c}_a(k) = \bar{c}_a(k)$ if $r_a(k) > 0$ and $\tilde{c}_a(k) = \bar{c}_b(k)$ otherwise, and likewise for $b$. A limit that only one configuration reaches thus contributes only to $\rho$, and a limit that neither reaches contributes nothing. This is an accounting convention: where one configuration returns no subset, nothing is known about how well it would predict there, and the convention does not estimate it. The reach part includes limits that $b$ reaches in fewer seeds than $a$, not only limits that it never reaches. Over the datasets $i$ of a group, we report two summaries:
\begin{equation}
  S = \frac{\sum_i \rho_i}{\sum_i \Delta_i},
  \qquad
  A = \frac{\sum_i |\rho_i|}{\sum_i |\rho_i| + \sum_i |\kappa_i|}.
  \label{eq:shares}
\end{equation}
$S$ is the share of the net AUF difference due to reach. It can exceed one when quality parts are negative. $A$ is the share of the total size of both parts and lies between zero and one. Both get 95\% bootstrap intervals over datasets.

To compare quality without the effect of reach, we compare $a$ and $b$ only at limits that both reach. Let $M$ be the set of limits $k \leq K$ with $r_a(k) \geq 0.6$ and $r_b(k) \geq 0.6$, that is, reached in at least three of five seeds. The \emph{matched-size difference} on a dataset is $m = |M|^{-1} \sum_{k \in M} \bigl(\bar{c}_a(k) - \bar{c}_b(k)\bigr)$, undefined when $M$ is empty. The two means in it may come from different seeds, so we also report a seed-paired version: for each seed $s$, it averages $q_{a,s}(k) - q_{b,s}(k)$ over the limits in $M$ that both runs with that seed reach, and then averages over seeds. We repeat the comparison with subsets of exactly $k$ features to check that the result does not depend on the at-most-$k$ convention. These quantities are accounting steps for reporting results, not new measures.

A run's returned front is not the same as everything the run evaluated. A small subset can be evaluated, kept for some generations, and later replaced in the population. We therefore read the time at which a run first reaches the limit from its archive, the best subset of each size among all evaluated subsets, and we read reach at the end from the returned front. In all our runs, the archive and the returned front agree at the end of the run on whether the limit of ten features is reached.

\subsection{Access to Small Subsets at Initialization}
\label{sec:access}

Whether the initial population already contains a small subset depends on how it is built. The opposition-based initialization used in NSGAII-MIIP~\cite{Li2025} builds 50 pairs for a population of 100. The first member of a pair selects each feature independently with probability one half, and the second selects exactly the features the first does not. If the first member has $m$ features, $m$ follows a binomial distribution with $d$ trials and probability one half, and its partner has $d - m$. A pair contains a non-empty subset with at most $K$ features with probability
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{pair}} = \Pr\left(1 \leq m \leq K \ \text{or}\ 1 \leq d - m \leq K\right),
  \qquad m \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(d, \tfrac{1}{2}),
  \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}
and the population contains one with probability $1 - (1 - p_{\mathrm{pair}})^{50}$. For $K = 10$, this probability is 1 for 16 and 24 features, 0.43 for 36, 0.11 for 40, and below 0.01 from 46 features on. Beyond a few dozen features, the opposition-based search must therefore produce every small subset through crossover, mutation, and selection.

The sparse initialization draws each subset size uniformly from 1 to $\min(30, d)$ and then selects that many features at random, the size sampling described by Miyandoab et al.~\cite{Miyandoab2024} with a narrower range. For $d \geq 30$, each member has at most ten features with probability $1/3$, so a population of 100 lacks such a member with probability $(2/3)^{100} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-18}$. On the two datasets with fewer features, PenDigits (16) and ItalyPowerDemand (24), the range is cut at $d$ and the probability is $10/d$, larger than $1/3$. The sparse start therefore reaches the limit at the start, whatever the number of features.

The pilot of Section~\ref{sec:pilot} adds two starts that cover all sizes from 1 to $d$. The \emph{uniform start} draws the size uniformly from 1 to $d$, as in the initial population of Miyandoab et al.~\cite{Miyandoab2024}; a member then has at most $K$ features with probability $K/d$. The \emph{log-uniform start} draws size $k$ with probability proportional to $1/k$; a member has at most $K$ features with probability $H_K / H_d$, where $H_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} 1/i$. For $K = 10$ and a population of 100, the uniform start contains such a member with probability 0.997 for 176 features, 0.75 for 720, and 0.18 for 5{,}000, while for the log-uniform start the probability per member is still 0.32 at 5{,}000 features, so the population contains one with near certainty.

\subsection{How Often Mutation Changes a Subset}
\label{sec:mutation}

The balanced mutation of NSGAII-MIIP~\cite{Oh2004,Li2025} removes each selected feature with probability $\mu$ and adds each unselected feature with probability $\mu k / (d - k)$, where $k$ is the current subset size. With the rate $\mu = 1/d$ used here and $0 < k < d$, the expected numbers of removed and added features are both $k/d$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}[\text{removed}] = k \cdot \frac{1}{d} = \frac{k}{d},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{E}[\text{added}] = (d - k) \cdot \frac{k}{d(d - k)} = \frac{k}{d}.
  \label{eq:flips}
\end{equation}
The expected change in size is therefore zero, as intended, and the expected number of changed features is $2k/d$. For a small subset in a wide dataset, this is very small. Because each feature changes independently with a small probability, the number of changed features is close to a Poisson count with mean $2k/d$, and a child is left unchanged by mutation with probability close to $e^{-2k/d}$. With $d = 10{,}000$ and $k = 10$, the expected number of changes is 0.002, and a child is left unchanged with probability 0.998. For comparison, ordinary bit-flip mutation at the same rate changes one feature on average and changes the expected size by $1 - 2k/d$. At the boundaries, a full subset ($k = d$) can only lose features, each with probability $1/d$, and an empty one ($k = 0$) never changes. No repair step follows crossover or mutation, so a child can be empty; it is then scored as in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. In the pilot, 0.03 to 7\% of the proposed subsets per configuration were empty.

Crossover cannot compensate when the parents are similar. The single-point crossover used here exchanges the parents' tails after a position at which they differ, so the two children together keep the parents' total number of selected features, and two identical parents produce identical children. Once a sparse population contains many copies of a few small subsets, most children therefore repeat subsets that have already been evaluated. A repeated subset is taken from the cache and costs no model fit, so the run proposes many subsets for each new fit.

Repeats therefore have two sources: children that mutation leaves unchanged, and children that differ from their parents but equal a subset evaluated earlier. A mutation that always changes at least one feature removes the first source but not the second. Once the population has converged on a few small subsets, a single change to one of them often gives a neighbor that has already been evaluated. The pilot tests both parts with such a mutation (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}). This account follows from the operators; it describes a mechanism that the results in Section~\ref{sec:results} are consistent with, not a proof that it is the only cause.
