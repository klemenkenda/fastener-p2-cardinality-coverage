\subsection{The AUF Difference Is a Difference in Reach}
\label{sec:res-reach}

\input{tables/criterion}

With a limit of ten features, the sparse start has a much higher AUF than the opposition-based (OBL) start: in the three benchmark groups, the mean difference per dataset is 0.42 to 0.49. Table~\ref{tab:criterion} shows where this difference comes from. On no dataset in any group does the OBL start reach the limit in more seeds than the sparse start. The ties are datasets with few features, on which both starts always reach it. The mean difference in reach is 0.54 to 0.85, with Holm-corrected $p \leq 0.016$ and rank-biserial correlation 1 in every group.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_reach_quality}
  \caption{The split of the AUF difference between the two starts is dominated by reach. Each pair of points joined by a line is one dataset: the part of the sparse start's AUF lead due to reaching more limits (reach part) and the part due to better scores at the limits both reach (quality part). Test split, seed means, limit of ten features}
  \label{fig:reach-quality}
\end{figure}

Splitting each dataset's AUF difference into a reach part and a quality part (Eq.~\ref{eq:split}; Fig.~\ref{fig:reach-quality}) shows that the split is dominated by reach. The two summaries measure different things. The reach part is 100.0 to 101.2\% of the net AUF difference ($S$ in Table~\ref{tab:criterion}), and 98.1 to 99.8\% of the combined absolute size of the reach and quality parts ($A$); the lowest lower bound of the intervals is 95.2\%, for $A$ under the random forest. Most of the reach part comes from limits that the OBL start never reaches on a dataset: they account for 88 to 99\% of the net difference, and the rest comes from limits that it reaches in fewer seeds than the sparse start. The quality parts lie close to zero on almost every dataset. The two clear exceptions, below zero under the random forest, are BeetleFly and Coffee, the datasets with 8 and 11 test rows, which is why $S$ exceeds 100\% there.

Sizes that both starts reach occur on only 1 to 13 datasets per group, with at most 512 features (Table~\ref{tab:criterion}b). Under the decision tree and LightGBM, the mean difference in test F1 at matched sizes is $-0.001$ and $+0.001$, with 95\% intervals within $\pm 0.02$. Under the random forest, it is $-0.014$ with the interval $[-0.044, +0.008]$, which exceeds the margin, so the primary analysis is inconclusive for this classifier. BeetleFly ($-0.106$) and Coffee ($-0.138$), both in favor of the OBL start, cause this. Without the datasets with at most 50 test rows, which leaves 11 datasets with at most 235 features, the mean is $+0.006$ $[+0.000, +0.013]$. This rule was chosen with the primary result in view, so the result is an exploratory stability check, not a confirmation. Besides BeetleFly and Coffee, individual datasets differ more than the group means (Supplementary Table~S3): Adiac ($+0.028$) and TwoLeadECG ($+0.022$) under the random forest and Adiac ($+0.036$) under LightGBM exceed 0.02 in favor of the sparse start. The seed-paired means differ from the conditional means by at most 0.0011 in the primary analysis and 0.0013 in the exploratory one, so they do not change the interpretation; subsets of exactly $k$ features give the same picture. The designed experiment has a single matched dataset, satimage ($-0.007$), from which no general statement follows. Nothing is known about quality at matched sizes on wider datasets, because there the OBL start returns no subset to compare.

\subsection{Reach Depends on the Number of Features}
\label{sec:res-d}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_reach_vs_d}
  \caption{The opposition-based search reaches ten features on datasets with up to about 100 features and almost never beyond 500, while the sparse start always does. Each point is one dataset: the share of its five runs whose returned front holds a subset with at most ten features; points are offset slightly by configuration. The grey curve is the probability that an opposition-based initial population already holds such a subset (Eq.~\ref{eq:pair})}
  \label{fig:reach-d}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:reach-d} relates reach to the number of features. The initial opposition-based population holds a subset with at most ten features only on PenDigits (16 features), ItalyPowerDemand (24), and in one seed of satimage (36), as the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:access} predicts. Selection then brings the front to the limit within the budget on every dataset with at most 99 features. Between 152 and 512 features, some datasets reach the limit and others do not, and beyond 512 features none does. A logistic model puts the number of features at which half of the OBL runs reach the limit at 163 $[122, 202]$, 212 $[145, 277]$, and 226 $[148, 300]$ under the three classifiers of the benchmark and at 172 $[92, 174]$ in the designed experiment. For NSGAII-MIIP, the same point lies at 208 $[159, 253]$, 268 $[224, 343]$, and 266 $[210, 302]$ features. These are fitted locations for these datasets and budgets, not thresholds that carry over to other benchmarks. The budgets were set per dataset and grow with the number of features, so the model cannot separate the two, and the intervals for the budget coefficient are wide. We therefore do not claim a separate effect of the budget from these runs; the pilot and the confirmatory test vary the budget directly (Sections~\ref{sec:res-pilot} and~\ref{sec:res-confirm}).

The sparse start reaches the limit in all 594 of its runs. Every one of its returned fronts holds a subset with a single feature.

\subsection{When the Limit Is Reached}
\label{sec:res-time}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_time_to_reach}
  \caption{The sparse start holds a subset with at most ten features from its first generation, while most opposition-based runs never reach one. Kaplan--Meier curves of the share of runs whose archive holds such a subset, against the share of the fit budget spent (logarithmic). Runs within a group are pooled for display; the tests in the text are per dataset}
  \label{fig:time}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:time} shows when runs first reach the limit. All runs of the sparse start reach it with the initial population. Of the opposition-based runs, 12.5\%, 6.7\%, 6.5\%, and 9.0\% have reached it after 10\% of the budget, in the designed experiment and under the decision tree, random forest, and LightGBM, and 15\%, 30\%, 36\%, and 48\% by the end. NSGAII-MIIP reaches it in 37\%, 46\%, and 56\% of its runs by the end. The restricted mean time to reach is shorter for the sparse start than for the OBL start on every dataset (rank-biserial correlation $-1$), by 0.72 to 0.86 of the budget on average, and shorter than for NSGAII-MIIP by 0.66 to 0.72. No run lost a small subset once its archive held one: in all 1{,}544 runs, the returned front reaches the limit exactly when the archive does.

\subsection{The Cost of a Sparse Start}
\label{sec:res-cost}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_large_end}
  \caption{The sparse start gives up the large subsets. (a) Per dataset, the best test F1 at any size of the sparse start minus that of the opposition-based start, by group (tree: decision tree; forest: random forest); bars are means, vertical lines their 95\% bootstrap intervals. (b) The largest subset on the returned front, median over seeds and groups, with $d/2$ for reference. Test split}
  \label{fig:cost}
\end{figure}

The sparse start does not search better in general; it searches elsewhere. Without a size limit, the best subset on its front, chosen on validation, has a lower test F1 than the best subset of the opposition-based start on most datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}a). The mean difference is $-0.007$ under the decision tree (7 wins, 23 losses; Holm $p = 0.013$), $-0.029$ under the random forest (5 wins, 29 losses; $p < 0.001$), and $-0.022$ under LightGBM (3 wins, 1 tie, 24 losses; $p < 0.001$). Against NSGAII-MIIP the difference is $-0.012$ to $-0.021$. In the designed experiment, the sparse start has the lower best score on five of the eight datasets.

The reason is visible in the sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}b). The fronts of the sparse start end at no more than 36 features on any dataset, while those of the opposition-based start and of NSGAII-MIIP reach close to half of all features on the wide datasets. The median difference in the largest subset is 97, 69, and 28 features under the three classifiers. The sparse start still leads at larger limits: its AUF is higher by 0.40 to 0.48 with a limit of 20 features, and by 0.08 to 0.14 when AUF is taken over the whole front ($K = d$). The pilot shows that a start covering all sizes does not remove this cost (Section~\ref{sec:res-pilot}).

\subsection{An Informed Improvement Phase Does Not Close the Gap}
\label{sec:res-miip}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_three_arms}
  \caption{The improvement phase of our NSGAII-MIIP implementation adds little reach. Per dataset, the difference in the share of runs that reach ten features: (a) NSGAII-MIIP minus the opposition-based start, (b) the sparse start minus NSGAII-MIIP; bars are means, vertical lines their 95\% bootstrap intervals}
  \label{fig:miip}
\end{figure}

Our implementation of NSGAII-MIIP starts from the same OBL population and adds the mutual-information improvement phase. Compared with the opposition-based start alone, its share of runs that reach the limit is higher by 0.043, 0.075, and 0.082 under the three classifiers (Holm $p = 0.125$, $0.016$, $0.062$), and its AUF by 0.058, 0.057, and 0.083 ($p = 0.005$, $0.031$, $0.029$). The sparse start is ahead of it by 0.64, 0.54, and 0.46 in reach and by 0.44, 0.39, and 0.33 in AUF (Fig.~\ref{fig:miip}). In these runs, the improvement phase therefore helps slightly, while the start matters far more for whether the front reaches small sizes.

\subsection{Repeated Subsets}
\label{sec:res-repeats}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_repeats}
  \caption{Almost all subsets proposed in sparse runs had been evaluated before. Per dataset, the median over seeds of the share of proposed subsets that were taken from the cache, against the number of features. NSGAII-MIIP is not shown, because its improvement phase evaluates subsets outside the generation count}
  \label{fig:repeats}
\end{figure}

The sparse start spends its proposals differently (Fig.~\ref{fig:repeats}). Over all runs of a group, the median share of proposed subsets that had already been evaluated is 0.96 to 0.99 for the sparse start and 0.10 to 0.18 for the OBL start, depending on the group. This agrees with the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:mutation}: in a population of small subsets, mutation rarely changes a child, and crossover between similar parents rarely produces a new subset. The pilot measures the first part directly (Section~\ref{sec:res-pilot}). Under the random forest and LightGBM, the stopping rule of 50 generations without a new subset ended 72 of 195 and 63 of 194 sparse runs, at a median of 8.8\% and 7.9\% of the budget, and 10 of 170 and 9 of 134 opposition-based runs, at 75\% and 59\%. Every stopped run had already reached the limit. The runs with the decision tree have no such rule, but they also differ in classifier, hardware, and code version, so the comparison between groups does not isolate the effect of the rule.

\subsection{Pilot: Budget, Starts over All Sizes, and a Forced Flip}
\label{sec:res-pilot}

The pilot (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) did not support any of its three registered claims, but each result sharpens how the main results should be read.

\paragraph{The opposition-based start reaches the limit with more budget.}
\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_pilot_budget}
  \caption{With enough fits, the opposition-based start reaches ten features on every pilot dataset; the fits it needs grow with the number of features. Kaplan--Meier curves of the share of the five 10$\times$ runs whose archive holds a subset with at most ten features, against fits in multiples of the benchmark budget. The 1$\times$ and 3$\times$ runs are the same trajectories cut short. Validation split}
  \label{fig:pilot-budget}
\end{figure}
The share of opposition-based runs whose front reaches ten features at 1$\times$, 3$\times$, and 10$\times$ the budget is 1.0, 1.0, and 1.0 on Adiac, 0.2, 1.0, and 1.0 on Strawberry, and 0.0, 0.0, and 1.0 on both Earthquakes and Computers (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot-budget}). The registered expectation that reach would stay incomplete at 10$\times$ on the two widest datasets therefore fails. The archive first holds a subset with at most ten features after a median of 6{,}534, 9{,}292, 47{,}830, and 85{,}239 fits, that is, 0.54, 1.55, 3.99, and 7.10 times the benchmark budget, or 37, 40, 93, and 118 fits per feature. The effort grows with the number of features on these four datasets, and so do the fits per feature, but four datasets that also differ in other respects cannot show how it scales; the confirmatory test does (Section~\ref{sec:res-confirm}). Once it gets there, the OBL start scores similarly to the sparse start: at 10$\times$ its AUF is 0.009 higher than that of the sparse start at 1$\times$ (higher in 12 of 20 seeds), at a median run time of 1{,}480 to 4{,}691 seconds against 216 to 907. On these four datasets, the boundary of Section~\ref{sec:res-d} is thus one of budget: at a fixed budget, AUF with a small limit largely measures how far the start is from the small sizes.

\paragraph{Starts over all sizes keep reach but not the large subsets.}
\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_pilot_starts}
  \caption{Starts that cover all sizes score similarly to the sparse start, not better. Per dataset and seed, the uniform and log-uniform starts minus the sparse start with the same seed: (a) best test F1 at any size, (b) AUF with a limit of ten features; bars are means. Test split}
  \label{fig:pilot-starts}
\end{figure}
\input{tables/pilot_fronts}
Both starts that cover sizes 1 to $d$ reach ten features in every run. Their best test F1 at any size is at least that of the sparse start in only 7 and 8 of 20 seeds (mean differences $-0.014$ and $-0.005$), and their AUF differs from it by 0.000 and $-0.002$ on average (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot-starts}). The reason is in the fronts (Table~\ref{tab:pilot-fronts}). Whatever the start, the population ends on a few small subsets, and the large subsets that a wide start contains at the beginning do not survive. With ten times the budget, the OBL start also ends on small subsets once it has reached them. The loss at the large-subset end (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}) therefore arises during the search under the tested variation and survival rules, not from the start. We did not vary selection or crossover, so the pilot does not show which step removes the large subsets.

\paragraph{Repeats follow the $2k/d$ account, and a forced flip lowers their cost but not the quality.}
\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_pilot_unchanged}
  \caption{Balanced mutation leaves children unchanged at about the rate the analysis predicts. One point per run with balanced mutation: the share of children that mutation left unchanged, against $2k/d$ at the run's median size $k$ of newly evaluated subsets; the curve is $e^{-2k/d}$ (Section~\ref{sec:mutation}). An approximate check: the exact prediction would average $e^{-2k/d}$ over the sizes of all mutated children, which were not logged}
  \label{fig:pilot-unchanged}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_pilot_min1}
  \caption{A forced flip makes the small-subset searches far cheaper but not better. Per start, one point per run: (a) the share of proposed subsets that had already been evaluated, (b) proposals per new model fit (logarithmic), (c) AUF with the forced flip minus AUF with balanced mutation, same seed and initial population; bars are means}
  \label{fig:pilot-min1}
\end{figure}
The share of children that balanced mutation leaves unchanged lies close to $e^{-2k/d}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot-unchanged}): 0.94 to 0.99 per dataset for the three starts with small subsets and 0.48 to 0.85 for the opposition-based start. The forced flip lowers the share of repeated proposals in every seed, from a median of 0.94 to 0.73 for the sparse start and from 0.94 to 0.70 for the log-uniform start, but never by half, and it changes AUF little ($-0.003$ and $0.000$ on average; Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot-min1}). With the forced flip, a child rarely equals a member of the population (5\% of proposals, against 45\% with balanced mutation for the sparse start), yet 78\% of all proposals had already been evaluated: the second source of repeats of Section~\ref{sec:mutation}. What the forced flip changes is the cost. Pooled over its runs, the sparse start makes 4.7 proposals per new fit instead of 176, and its median run time on the four datasets falls from 290, 216, 847, and 907 seconds to 106, 28, 52, and 54. For the opposition-based start, whose population is still wide, the forced flip removes almost all repeats and raises AUF by 0.016.

\subsection{Confirmatory Test: The Fits Needed to Reach Ten Features}
\label{sec:res-confirm}

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{fig_confirm}
  \caption{On seven new datasets, the fits the opposition-based start needs to reach ten features grow faster than the number of features, as predicted from the pilot. Fits until the archive first holds a subset with at most ten features, one point per run; bars are medians over five seeds. The line is the registered prediction (Eq.~\ref{eq:confirm}), shaded within a factor of two; the horizontal lines mark the smallest and largest benchmark budgets. Validation split}
  \label{fig:confirm}
\end{figure}

The prediction of Section~\ref{sec:confirm} holds on all three registered parts (Table~\ref{tab:confirm}, Fig.~\ref{fig:confirm}). All 35 runs reached ten features before the cap, none after more than half of it. The medians lie between 0.95 and 1.59 times the prediction, so all seven datasets are within the factor of two; of the single runs, only one, on Isolet, falls just outside it (2.02 times). The slope over the seven datasets is 1.70 $[1.61, 1.82]$, well above 1, so the fits grow faster than in proportion to $d$. The interval does not include the predicted exponent of 1.88, which was estimated on four datasets. The prediction was too low on six of the seven datasets, by a factor of 1.24 on geometric average, most on the datasets with the fewest features (1.53 to 1.59 at 128 to 174 features) and least on those with the most (0.95 to 1.19 at 970 to 1{,}024). Over the eleven datasets of the pilot and the test together, the slope is 1.75 $[1.63, 1.93]$.

The numbers show how far the benchmark budgets were from what this start needs. At about 1{,}000 features, it needed 154{,}000 to 214{,}000 fits before it held a subset with ten features, while the benchmark budgets were 2{,}000 to 12{,}000. On the line fitted to the test, a dataset with 5{,}000 features, such as gisette, would need about 2.6 million fits, but this lies beyond the tested range. The test cannot separate the number of features from the number of training rows. The three datasets with the fewest features also have the most rows (8{,}346 to 30{,}002), and the ratio to the prediction rises with the rows (Spearman correlation 0.77) as it falls with $d$ ($-0.89$). Because each run stops when it reaches the limit, the test also says nothing about the scores of the opposition-based start once it is there.

The robustness of the main results to runs that did not finish and to the choice of classifier is shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}.
